Why Economic Crises Repeat

Economic crises don’t repeat because people forget history.

They repeat because the underlying incentives never change.

The structure is simple: opportunity → capital → excess → collapse.

What changes is the form it takes.

In the past decade, we’ve seen this play out in very different environments.

Take the cryptocurrency boom of 2020–2022.

The original idea had real value — decentralized finance, removal of intermediaries, and a new financial architecture.

But as capital entered the space, the focus shifted.

Projects were no longer evaluated on utility, but on momentum.

Tokens with no underlying value reached massive valuations. Retail investors entered late, driven by the fear of missing out.

And when confidence dropped, the entire system unwound rapidly.

Not because the idea failed — but because speculation had overtaken it.

A similar pattern appeared in the startup ecosystem during the era of near-zero interest rates.

Cheap capital led to aggressive funding. Startups were valued based on growth projections rather than sustainable economics.

“Scale first, figure out profits later” became the dominant strategy.

As long as liquidity was available, the model held.

But once interest rates rose and capital tightened, the weaknesses became visible.

Valuations corrected. Funding slowed. And companies built on unsustainable assumptions struggled.

On a completely different scale, the Sri Lankan economic crisis followed a similar structure.

Years of fiscal mismanagement, heavy borrowing, and policy decisions disconnected from economic reality created a fragile system.

When external shocks hit — declining tourism and rising import costs — the system couldn’t sustain itself.

Shortages followed. Inflation rose. The economy collapsed.

Different sectors. Different causes.

Same pattern.

What connects these cases is not the asset or the policy.

It’s behavior.

When capital is easily available, risk-taking increases.

When prices rise, confidence grows.

And when confidence grows, people stop questioning assumptions.

That’s when systems become fragile.

Because stability, at that point, depends on conditions continuing exactly as they are.

The moment those conditions change, everything adjusts at once.

And that adjustment is what we call a crisis.

The reason crises repeat is not because we lack information.

It’s because incentives push individuals to act in ways that collectively create instability.

And unless those incentives change, the pattern will continue.
