Why I’m Building Arbor

Most startups begin with a market opportunity. A founder identifies a gap, sees demand, and decides to build a solution. Arbor began differently. It started with frustration. A few months ago, I began paying closer attention to my own finances. There was nothing particularly complicated about them. Some savings, some expenses, a growing interest in investing, and a desire to understand where my money was actually going. Naturally, I looked for tools that could help. There were plenty of options. Expense trackers, budgeting apps, investment dashboards. The problem was that none of them seemed to answer the question I actually cared about. They could tell me where my money had been. Very few could help me understand where it was likely to go.

At first, I assumed I simply had unrealistic expectations. Most people use financial tools to track spending, monitor accounts, and set budgets. Those are useful functions. But the more I explored, the more I noticed a deeper problem. Nearly every platform treated finances as something static. They recorded information. They organized information. Sometimes they visualized information. But they rarely helped users think about uncertainty. And uncertainty is arguably the most important part of finance. Nobody knows exactly what their future income will be. Nobody knows what markets will do. Nobody knows when unexpected expenses will appear. Financial decisions are made in a world of probabilities, yet most financial tools behave as though the future is predictable.

That observation led me to a simple question. What if personal finance software was designed around uncertainty instead of ignoring it? Instead of merely showing users where they are today, what if it helped them understand where they could be tomorrow? That idea became the foundation for Arbor. Rather than functioning as another expense tracker, I wanted it to behave more like a financial operating system. A system that connects assets, liabilities, investments, expenses, and income into a single framework. More importantly, a system that treats financial planning as a dynamic process rather than a static snapshot.

The concept became even more interesting when I started studying economics and financial systems more seriously. One of the biggest lessons economics teaches is that outcomes are rarely certain. Economies operate under uncertainty. Businesses operate under uncertainty. Investors operate under uncertainty. Yet many individuals make financial decisions using tools that fail to account for that reality. This creates a contradiction. People spend enormous amounts of time trying to predict the future, even though prediction is often impossible. A better approach is not predicting outcomes perfectly. It is understanding the range of outcomes that are realistically possible.

That idea led to some of the features I am most excited about. One example is the Financial Resilience Index, or FRI, a metric I have been developing to measure financial stability rather than financial wealth. Traditional financial metrics often focus on how much someone owns. The FRI asks a different question: how prepared is someone for uncertainty? A person with a large investment portfolio but unstable income may be less resilient than someone with fewer assets but greater financial stability. Wealth and resilience are related, but they are not the same thing. Understanding that distinction became central to the way I approached the project.

Of course, building Arbor has been far more difficult than imagining it. Ideas are always cleaner than execution. Every feature raises new questions. How should risk be measured? How should future outcomes be modeled? Which assumptions are realistic and which are misleading? The deeper I go, the more complexity I uncover. Yet that challenge is exactly what makes the project interesting. Arbor sits at the intersection of many subjects I care about: economics, finance, entrepreneurship, data science, and technology. Building it has forced me to think about all of those disciplines simultaneously.

Looking back, I do not think Arbor is really about personal finance. At least not entirely. The deeper goal is helping people understand uncertainty. Most financial decisions are ultimately decisions about the future, and the future is never guaranteed. The best financial systems are not the ones that predict perfectly. They are the ones that help people navigate uncertainty intelligently. Whether Arbor succeeds or fails, that idea has already changed the way I think about money. We spend so much time measuring wealth that we often forget to measure resilience. Arbor exists because I believe the second question may be the more important one.
