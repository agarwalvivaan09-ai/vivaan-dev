Why I’m Building Arbor

Recently, I started managing my own money.

It wasn’t anything complex — just pocket money, basic expenses, and small decisions about where to spend and where to save.

But I wanted to track it properly.

So I looked for tools.

There were plenty of expense trackers. Some were clean, some were detailed. But almost all of them had the same limitation.

They treated finances as static.

They could track what you spent. Sometimes what you earned. But they didn’t account for how money actually behaves.

Investments weren’t integrated properly. Risk wasn’t modeled. Future uncertainty wasn’t considered.

And that’s where the idea for Arbor came from.

I didn’t just want to track money.

I wanted to understand it.

Arbor is what I’m currently building — a financial operating system that goes beyond simple tracking.

It integrates assets, expenses, income, liabilities, and investments into one system.

It connects to real-world market data. It tracks multiple accounts. It accounts for interest rates, inflation, and compounding.

But more importantly, it tries to model uncertainty.

Using concepts like Monte Carlo simulations and stochastic modeling, it doesn’t just show where you are — it attempts to show where you could be.

Instead of a static snapshot, it creates a range of possible outcomes.

That’s the difference.

Because in reality, finances are not predictable.

They are probabilistic.

One part of the system I’ve been working on is something I call the Financial Resilience Index (FRI).

It’s an attempt to quantify how stable or fragile a financial position is, based on factors like income consistency, expenses, risk exposure, and external variables.

The idea is simple:

not just to measure wealth, but to measure stability.

Building Arbor has been a combination of everything I’ve been learning — computer science, economics, and an increasing interest in how financial systems actually behave.

It’s still a work in progress.

There’s a lot left to build, refine, and test.

But the goal is clear.

To create a system that doesn’t just record financial activity, but helps understand it in a dynamic, real-world context.

And if it works, it could be useful not just for individuals, but for households and small businesses as well.

That’s what I’m working toward.
