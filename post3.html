After My First Two Fundraisers

Our first fundraiser was called Tech Tonik.

The idea was simple — a techno-themed party. Music, games, energy. On paper, everything looked like it would work.

In reality, it was chaos.

We reached the venue two hours before the event, thinking we had enough time. We didn’t.

Decorations were incomplete. Setup was rushed. Things that should have been planned earlier were being figured out on the spot.

Then things got worse.

The DJ, who was supposed to arrive at 4:30, showed up at 5:30. The event was meant to start at 5. Music only began around 6:30.

We expected around 150 people. Around 70 showed up.

Financially, we barely covered our costs.

There was confusion. There was stress. There was a lot of shouting.

But there was something else too.

It was fun.

It was our first real attempt at building something like this, and despite everything going wrong, people still showed up, stayed, and enjoyed.

And that mattered.

But what came next mattered even more.

Our second fundraiser was called Retrograde.

On paper, it was a much bigger and better event. Planning was stronger, execution was tighter, and expectations were higher.

But the struggle behind it was ten times harder.

On the night before the event, I got a call saying that our venue had been cancelled due to a legal issue.

Everything was ready. Marketing was done. People were expecting the event. The stakes were much higher this time.

Cancelling wasn’t an option.

That night, my team and I stayed up trying to find a solution. After hours of searching, we finally managed to secure a new venue through a friend.

It was more expensive. It reduced our margins. But we didn’t have a choice.

The bigger problem was communication.

Everyone already knew the original venue. Now we had to inform people, convince them, and make sure they still showed up.

We split into teams. We called people individually. We followed up. We pushed until we were sure they knew.

And then we had to do everything again — setup, decor, coordination — from scratch, in a matter of hours.

Somehow, it worked.

Retrograde turned out to be a much stronger event. The energy was better. The experience was smoother. The execution felt controlled.

Looking back, these two events taught me more than anything else.

First, you can never afford to be underprepared. Problems don’t come one at a time — they come all at once.

Second, how you react matters more than what goes wrong. Panic doesn’t solve problems. Clear thinking does.

Third, the more you struggle, the more you learn. And often, the better the outcome becomes.

And finally, everything comes at a cost — whether it’s time, effort, or margin.

But if you’re willing to push through, things tend to work out.
