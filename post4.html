What Managing People Taught Me

Managing people sounds simple until you actually start doing it.

Everyone has different opinions, different expectations, and different ways of working. Conflicts are inevitable. Misunderstandings happen constantly.

And as a leader, you’re expected to handle all of it.

The first thing I learned was patience.

Not everything needs an immediate reaction. Not every conflict needs escalation. Sometimes, the best thing you can do is step back, listen, and let things settle before making a decision.

The second thing I learned is that you can’t pick sides.

The moment you start favouring one person over another, you lose trust. People begin to question your decisions. And once that happens, it’s very difficult to lead effectively.

Leadership requires you to stand by your decisions — even when they’re questioned.

If you don’t, you come across as uncertain. And no one respects a leader who isn’t sure of their own decisions.

I also learned that you can’t afford to get into conflicts with your own team.

When things go wrong, people don’t look for someone to argue with. They look for someone to stabilize the situation.

In difficult moments, your role is to be a pillar — not another source of chaos.

Another important lesson was around mistakes.

It’s okay to make them. Every leader does.

But repeating the same mistakes is what breaks trust.

People are willing to forgive errors. They are not willing to tolerate a lack of learning.

I also realized how dangerous ego can be.

The moment you start flaunting your position, everything changes. People stop being honest with you. They stop sharing real feedback.

And without honest feedback, you stop growing.

A leader who is respected but not feared will always build a stronger team than one who relies on authority.

Another thing I learned was to be careful about who you trust.

Some people chase positions. Others care about the work.

Over time, the difference becomes very clear.

The people who genuinely care will stay consistent. The ones chasing recognition won’t.

And then there’s feedback.

Not all feedback feels good. In fact, most of the useful feedback doesn’t.

But constructive criticism is one of the most important tools a leader has.

Some people will say nice things and mean the opposite. Others will be blunt — but honest.

Learning to tell the difference changes everything.

Managing people didn’t just teach me how to lead others.

It forced me to understand myself better — my decisions, my reactions, and my ability to stay consistent under pressure.

And more than anything, it taught me that leadership isn’t about control.

It’s about responsibility.
