The Psychology Behind Financial Bubbles

Financial bubbles don’t begin with irrationality.

They begin with something that makes sense.

A new opportunity. A growing market. A shift in technology. Something that genuinely has value.

And then, slowly, perception changes.

What starts as a reasonable investment turns into a belief that prices will keep rising indefinitely.

That’s where psychology takes over.

People stop asking whether something is worth its price. Instead, they start asking whether someone else will buy it at a higher price.

This is what drives a bubble.

Not fundamentals — but expectation.

The 2008 housing crisis is one of the clearest examples of this.

Housing, by itself, is a stable and necessary asset. It has real value. It serves a real purpose.

But over time, the narrative changed.

Housing prices kept rising, and people began to believe that they would never fall. Banks became more willing to lend. Risk standards dropped. More people entered the market.

And the cycle reinforced itself.

Rising prices created confidence. Confidence created more demand. More demand pushed prices even higher.

Until the system became fragile.

That’s the interesting part about bubbles — they look strongest just before they collapse.

Because by that point, everything depends on everything else.

Prices depend on demand. Demand depends on confidence. Confidence depends on prices continuing to rise.

The moment that chain breaks, the system unravels.

And when it does, it happens quickly.

The same factors that drove growth now work in reverse.

Falling prices reduce confidence. Reduced confidence lowers demand. Lower demand pushes prices down further.

What once looked stable turns out to be extremely fragile.

That’s the paradox of financial bubbles.

They are built slowly, but they collapse suddenly.

And at their core, they are less about markets and more about people.

Because markets don’t create bubbles.

Psychology does.
