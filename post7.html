What School Doesn’t Teach About Money

Economics in school is clean.

Demand curves slope downward. Supply curves slope upward. Markets move toward equilibrium. Everything behaves the way it’s supposed to.

But the real world doesn’t.

In reality, demand doesn’t always fall when prices rise. Supply doesn’t always respond predictably. Elasticity is never constant.

What we learn in school is a simplified version of how things work — a model, not reality.

And models only work as long as their assumptions hold.

The biggest assumption in economics is rational behavior.

But people aren’t always rational.

Decisions are influenced by fear, greed, uncertainty, and incomplete information. And once that happens, the system starts behaving very differently.

This is where theory begins to break down.

Take the Great Depression of 1929.

Classical economic theory assumed that markets would self-correct. If demand fell, prices would adjust, and the economy would recover naturally.

That didn’t happen.

Instead, the economy entered a prolonged period of decline. Unemployment rose, output fell, and markets failed to stabilize on their own.

It took entirely new thinking — Keynesian economics — to explain why government intervention was necessary.

A similar pattern appeared during the 2008 financial crisis.

Financial models assumed that risks were distributed and manageable. Markets were expected to function efficiently.

But those assumptions ignored human behavior.

Excessive risk-taking, overconfidence, and herd mentality drove the system far beyond what the models predicted.

When the system finally collapsed, policies struggled to keep up.

Because in moments like these, nothing behaves the way it does in textbooks.

Fiscal policy, monetary policy — all of it becomes harder to predict and harder to control.

Not because the tools are wrong, but because the environment is unstable.

And that’s the part school doesn’t teach.

Economics isn’t just about curves and equations.

It’s about people.

And people are unpredictable.

Which means the real world will always be more complex than the models we use to understand it.
